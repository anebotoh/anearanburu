01 — The project

The Searchable Museum

The National Museum of African American History and Culture (NMAAHC) is the Smithsonian's museum on the National Mall in Washington, D.C. — the world's largest museum dedicated to African American history and culture, with more than 40,000 objects in its collection.

The Searchable Museum: Slavery and Freedom prototype is a rich, online experience of the museum's Slavery and Freedom exhibition — a multimedia representation of its historical narratives, collections, and educational resources. The goal was to translate a physical exhibition into a digital one that lets virtual visitors explore at their own pace, follow their own interests, and preserve the narrative structure and tone of the gallery — while reaching people who may never get to walk through the museum's doors.

The goals

  • Improve access to the collection through an online resource for the exhibition.
  • Surface new, unexpected connections between the collection and the ideas the exhibition explores.
  • Expand the museum's reach beyond geographic barriers, toward more inclusive access for traditionally underserved communities.
  • Give researchers, teachers, and parents companion tools for deeper learning.
  • Build a flexible, sustainable system that could adapt the museum's other exhibitions into online experiences over time.

02 — My role

Leading the visual design

I worked as Lead Visual Designer. I owned the look and feel, the concept ideation, and the visual design of the prototype — taking the museum's brand elements and translating them into a visual concept for the digital experience: color, typography, imagery, iconography, and layout. That concept guided the rest of the design process, and from the approved wireframes and the selected look and feel I built the visual designs.

The result was a responsive website — desktop and mobile — where visitors could explore the different parts of the exhibition, built and refined through a series of prototypes we designed and tested.

The team

A focused, cross-disciplinary team — UX, visual, motion, 3D, and content strategy — with Fearless spearheading development. (Full credits are at the end.)

03 — The approach

Vision first, then an MVP

Phase 0

Pitch

Fantasy's initial pitch, with Fearless — four or five screens showing how the site could look, with no brand guidelines, framework, or rules yet.

Phase 1

Discovery — 2 months

Create a vision for what the site could be, and present it to the museum. We showed three directions — and they were too bold for the client.

Phase 2

MVP — 6 months

Defined the visual language and styleguide, produced the UX wireframes, and partnered with Fearless to build it.

Early on we didn't even know what this was — a microsite? something inside the existing system? did it follow the brand guidelines or not? Everyone had an opinion, and agreeing was hard. We started out designing for the whole museum, with its guidelines and an option to change them, then narrowed all the way down to a single exhibition: Slavery and Freedom. In all, we worked for eight months, hand in hand with the museum's curatorial team.

“Fantasy is known for doing ‘cool stuff.’ And now I had to design the Slavery and Freedom exhibition.”

04 — The personal challenge

The hardest part wasn't the design

The topic was serious, the team was small, and I felt the weight of it. As a non-American, non-Black person, with English not my first language, it was hard to lead the design at first — and my style leans European: minimal, restrained, not colorful.

During the project, the Black Lives Matter movement grew and the U.S. government changed — both of which directly touched the museum and the people who work there. So for the first two weeks, I mostly listened. I tried to understand how everyone communicated and to learn as much as I could — watching films, reading about African American culture, studying the use of color and shape. It didn't feel right to walk in and say, “here's how we're going to design this.”

Most of the museum's team were curators and historians; there was no design spokesperson on their side. That meant a lot of education in both directions — which, in the end, made every decision more thoughtful and more coherent.

05 — Design

Building the visual language

The core concept

An object constellation

The heart of the site was a living constellation: objects from the exhibition float in the middle of the screen and rearrange as you move, surfacing the connections between them. It let people explore history in any order — “explore history your way” — instead of following one fixed path. The constellation adapts all the way down to mobile, from an intro screen to a grid you drag and drop through.

A global framework

Built to hold the whole museum

We designed a framework versatile enough to adapt to every exhibition at the NMAAHC — History, Community, and Culture — even though our focus was Slavery and Freedom. Every exhibition in the museum is reachable from a single global menu, and each opens with an introduction from its curator (for Slavery and Freedom, Mary Elliott), broken into chapters that give the context a gallery guide would.

Motion with meaning

The elevator experience

The museum's real building has an elevator that descends by decade until you reach the year an exhibition begins. We recreated that feeling: the transition from the navigation into a landing page emulates the museum elevator — counting down the decades to Today — so the digital journey carries a piece of the physical one.

Look & feel

Three directions, and a hard conversation about color

We explored three directions: one aligned to the museum's existing brand; one rooted in the history of African American design and pop culture — jazz record covers; and one around Afrofuturism. The team liked the thinking, but color was the hard conversation. On such a delicate topic, the exhibition curator felt color wasn't appropriate — so we landed on an editorial, black-and-white treatment, in keeping with the exhibition's own aesthetic.

Typography

The museum's own typeface, sharpened

We kept the museum's typeface — Freight (FreightDisplay Pro and FreightSans Pro), designed by the first African American type designer — swapped the variants, and chose a display cut so it read sharper and more elegant.

Color system

Black-and-light, with color as a signal

The museum's brand runs on two colors, purple and orange. Because the framework had to extend to future exhibitions, we built a black-and-light site and used color to highlight and to identify each exhibition. We nudged the orange a little brighter, to feel younger, fresher, more modern.

Photography

One accent, so the imagery could breathe

The imagery skews sepia and black-and-white — which is exactly why we kept a single accent color, the orange, to make key moments pop.

A hard constraint

Designing with (almost) no imagery

Because of copyrights, the museum couldn't send us most images — and we couldn't manipulate the ones we did have. So the system had to hold up with minimal, fixed imagery: a strict 8px corner radius and a small set of aspect ratios (16:9, 2:3, 3:2) that kept everything consistent, no matter what any given exhibition could provide.

3D

Objects you can explore yourself

We recreated five objects from the exhibition in 3D — like the King Cotton Tower — so visitors could turn them over and study the details on their own, the way you never can behind museum glass.

A bulletproof system

One system for every kind of content

The exhibition threw every content type at us — objects, documents, audio, video, images with and without backgrounds, long titles, cropped transcriptions. So we bulletproofed the asset system: one flexible tile that handled all of it, with idle, hover, and inactive states, and cards with enough personality to adapt to whatever they had to show.

Stories

A timeline built for younger audiences

We built the stories as an interactive timeline — keeping the text as short as we could to earn engagement from younger visitors — so you can watch a concept like Land and Housing evolve across the years, from Reconstruction to today's gentrification.

Search & filters

An editorial search

Even the search was designed to feel editorial rather than utilitarian — surfacing people, stories, and places with the same care as the rest of the site, and filters that read more like a reading list than a form.

Foundations

An 8px design system, explained like a geek

I spent a couple of weeks preparing a presentation on how we'd work — our process, and how we'd tackle the project from a very technical point of view. It was deliberately geeky, and it needed to be: we were building a design system that could translate to the museum's other exhibitions, for stakeholders who weren't especially web-savvy. Throughout, I tried to stay technical — explaining the rationale behind every type size, color, and layout — so decisions never came down to “I like this.”

The system was built on a few principles:

  • Scalable
  • Consistent
  • Re-usable design patterns
  • Extensible
  • Platform-agnostic

What I heard afterward“Please don't apologize for being a geek — we work in a museum.” It felt amazing.

06 — Success

Reachable beyond the museum's doors

For the prototype to matter, it had to be adaptable and accessible — a foundation the museum and other history and culture institutions, researchers, and learners could build on, across many different experiences.

That was the point of the whole thing: to make the museum reachable not only for the people who visit, but for the people who may never have the chance to walk through its doors.

It went live as the Searchable Museum — you can explore it at searchablemuseum.com.
