Challenge 03

Launch and go to market

We were ready to launch when a call with Yamaha changed the plan — they wanted Creator aimed at instrumentalists new to production. So we made recording a core part of the experience and prepared to go to market together.

The audience

Creator has always served three kinds of musician — but who we designed for first changed over the course of the project. The beginner needs it to be easy enough to build a song and gain confidence with the language of music. The instrumentalist wants to record in seconds and have the palette match their take. And the pro just needs to sketch an idea fast and export stems into their own DAW.

At the very beginning, we focused almost entirely on the beginner. About a year and a half in, a partnership with Yamaha shifted the emphasis — their audience is full of instrumentalists, so the target moved toward musicians who already play but are new to producing. That's what the latest version responds to.

None of that was a one-time decision — the audience kept moving with the business, and we kept testing it. We interviewed people constantly, checking three things at once: whether there was real product–market fit, whether the thing was actually usable, and whether what we were trying to communicate through the design was landing the way we intended.

Recording

Making recording a first-class way in

We were weeks from launch when a conversation with Yamaha changed the trajectory. They saw the product and wanted it — but for their audience: instrumentalists who already play, yet have never produced. People who can pick up a guitar or sit at a keyboard, and still find a DAW intimidating.

That reframed the core experience. A beginner starts from sounds; an instrumentalist starts from their instrument. So we made recording one of the primary ways into a song — capture a take in seconds, and let Creator match the palette and build around what you played, instead of asking you to learn the tool first.

It kept the principle that ran through the whole redesign: meet people where they already are. The beginner builds by ear; the instrumentalist builds from their playing. Same product, two doors in — and recording became the one that opened Creator to an audience Yamaha already had.

Leadership & collaboration

Reducing friction across the org

Design leadership here was as much about the organization as the screens. A lot of the job was undoing inherited decisions and working around a rigid back-end IA — early on the app had to follow the broken logic of the system it fed from, and I had to earn the room to break those rules and build on top of them — all while the brand was mid-rework and the product was in constant discovery.

I leaned on AI throughout: prototyping concepts and mash-ups, pressure-testing workflow decisions, and shipping front-end edits in Cursor. And I helped carry the work into the world — presenting and demoing at NAMM and SXSW with Yamaha, watching strangers use the app, have fun with it, and show us exactly where it broke.

What I learned

Designing screen choreographies

Music is invisible. I was designing for abstract meaning — for the conversation between a piece of technology and a musician — constantly deciding how much the computer should do for you, and what creativity even means for a beginner. How do you encourage someone without doing it all for them? (At one point the honest question on the table was literally “how do you manage silence?” — we copy-pasted silence.)

A lot of the value was getting paid to ask the dumb questions with no shame: what does “intuitive” actually mean? what makes a design “smart”? Nothing was ever finished — it was continuous discovery. Set a vision, form a hypothesis, test it, fail, learn, repeat.

And I think the truest measure of Creator is what happens after it. It's a place to start, gain confidence, and graduate — to more advanced tools, to Output's other products, or, through Yamaha's program for instrumentalists, to a real path into producing your own music. It opens the door and points you somewhere good.

Impact

Impact 01

Aligning builders and stakeholders behind a new audience

The company had built its tools for pros and now wanted to open a beginners' market — which raised a real fear about how that would land with existing customers. A lot of the work was getting everyone, builders and stakeholders alike, aligned and genuinely agreeing on what we were building. That alignment took enormous effort, and it's what let the product move forward.

Impact 02

A partnership that positioned the product

The work earned a partnership with Yamaha — putting Output and Creator in the company of a legendary, globally trusted brand. They invited us to present and demo Creator at NAMM and SXSW, inside their own offering: an endorsement that told the market Creator was to be taken seriously.

Impact 03

A tool that's easy, fun, and builds confidence

Above all, the outcome is a music-production tool that's genuinely easy, fun, and engaging — one that teaches you how a song is actually made. It builds confidence: the sense that you understand the parts worth paying attention to, that you can build and finish your first song, and have fun doing it.
