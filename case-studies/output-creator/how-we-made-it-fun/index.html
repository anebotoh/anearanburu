Challenge 02

How we made it fun

Then we rebuilt it to play: reorganizing sounds by what they do instead of by instrument, and tuning the back-end algorithms — testing our way to the imperfect matches that made creating genuinely fun.

Learnings & Decisions

Learning 01

A good onboarding won't fix a UX problem

When I joined, the team was “improving the onboarding” to fix retention — our metric was first-two-day retention. We tried prompting (“what do you want to create?”), sound “starters,” and idea starters. The learning: beginners don't know what to prompt. They'd type three words, usually lifted from the examples we showed. Asking a beginner “what do you want?” doesn't help — you have to give them a framework that's easy to understand and fun to use. And making an app “smart” is far harder than it sounds; our smart-composition experiments proved it.

Learning 02

Get to the magic moment as fast as possible

We realized the first magic moment was the instant you layered two sounds and a vibe appeared. The whole job was getting people there sooner. We built song ideas — a modal that mixed a few sounds into an idea you could drop onto the timeline. Gabriel, one of our back-end engineers, wrote an algorithm to match complementary sounds — and the trick was to make it imperfect. On a 1–100 scale, the sweet spot was 35. At 99% the song was basically made for you, and that was boring. Imperfection left room to play.

Why it workedThere's a difference between making something easy and making it feel like it's yours. The imperfection is what made it feel like yours.

Design Decision 01

From instrument categories to sound functions

One of the decisions I'm proudest of: we changed the IA from instrument categories to sound-function categories. Instrument categories quietly told a beginner they had to already know something — an instrument, a genre, a sound type — before they could start. Organizing by what a sound does let anyone begin, no vocabulary required.

Design Decision 02

No sound titles — the ear as a guide

We went back and forth on whether to show sample names at all. My instinct was clean, scannable lists with solid IA. The PM was certain we should lead with sound, not names, so people build by ear instead of reading. He was right — and I'll say so plainly: his call was better than mine. Leading with sound made the app more fun and easier for every audience.

The lessonLeading well includes knowing when someone else's call beats yours — and getting out of its way.

Design Decision 03

Starters became dots

Instead of loading long lists of sounds per collection, we grouped them by sound type and surfaced them as dots on the first screen — a way to start an idea or a section, not the entire workflow at once. Paired with the one rule that held everything together — color means sound — the first screen went from intimidating to inviting.
