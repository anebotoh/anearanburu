Creator

Output — 2024–2026 · Desktop App

Rebuilding the creative core of Output Creator

Over two and a half years, I led the end-to-end redesign of Output Creator — from workflow architecture and interaction design to user research, visual design, micro-interactions, and front-end polish. It was designed using Figma and Cursor, but it was shaped by countless conversations, disagreements, iterations, and an ongoing rebrand. Together, we launched a product that was genuinely fun to use — and helped secure a partnership with Yamaha, including live demos at NAMM and SXSW.

Challenge 01

Make it understandable

We pulled the 1.0 apart — mapping where users got lost, auditing the visual hierarchy and layout, and learning that crisp, good-looking visuals rarely make a product easier to understand.

The project

Redesigning a creative workflow

I joined in January 2024. The company had launched the 1.0 version of Creator two months earlier and overall the feedback from users was that they didn't understand how it worked. The conceptual thinking was great, but along the way the product that shipped wasn't matching the initial thinking.

Over the next two and a half years I redesigned the app twice, designed more than 2,000 screens, tested countless prototypes — and became genuinely annoying to friends and family, running ideas past anyone who would listen.

Usable & intuitive

Start a song, finish a song

The biggest problem with the first prototype was blunt: people didn't know how to use it. They couldn't build their first song — they'd open it and stall.

So we set the goals in plain terms. How do we get someone to start a song, and actually finish one? How do we serve the right sounds at the right moment — remembering that underneath, Creator is a sample library, and every sound you place is pulled from it? And how do we make people feel, the whole way through, like they're learning rather than being carried?

That last question became its own thread. A big part of the work was educational — surfacing the parts of a song worth paying attention to as you build, so the product leaves you more capable than it found you, not just with a track to show for it.

My role

Ignorance is bliss

I was hired partly because I didn't know music production. I'm a lifelong music junkie and studied classical music when I was younger, but I'd downloaded Ableton three or four times and given up every time. On this project, not knowing was one of my best assets — the people who built the 1.0 version had too much experience with music production to remember how someone starts learning about it.

The version 1.0 was a year of decisions taken with no product design lens or beginner point of view. So I spent the first month stripping them back and building a point of view — cataloguing the usability, visual-design, interaction, and information-architecture problems one by one. Then we got everyone in a room — in person — to agree on a direction before I designed a single new screen.

Tools & process

As Lead Product Designer I owned the product design end to end — from workflow architecture and interaction design through the visual design system and micro-interactions. I set the design concept, designed all the flows, ran critiques, and drove alignment across a cross-functional squad, reporting into Neil Halimen (VP Creative) and partnering closely with Daniel Lepik on 3D and visual design.

The process itself kept changing across the two years, as we adopted AI tools along the way. What started in Figma ended up in Cursor — I could write PR tickets and take on front-end polish work myself, getting the final product to a more finished, polished place without spending developer time.

Learning by doing & teamwork

Being a beginner in music production while designing a tool for beginners meant I was basically my own experiment. I was learning as I designed — asking every dumb question you can think of.

The team were seasoned musicians who'd been building tools for pros for years — so the first version quietly assumed a beginner just needs less, when really they need something different. And making something easy is far harder than it sounds: dumb it down and you strip out the essence; leave it whole and no one can get in. A lot of the work was long conversations reframing what it even means to learn to make music — and resisting the easy out of “this'll be enough for a beginner.”

Being the actual beginner in the room forced everyone to hold that lens. I was learning on two fronts at once — decoding what was happening on the screen, and decoding the craft underneath it: what a song actually needs, what the practical process of building one looks like, what to show first and what to leave out. Less music theory, more practicality — what makes sense, what doesn't, and why.

And that's where teamwork mattered. Coming from the agency world, I learned at Output what collaboration actually means. Everyone here has their own expertise — deeply skilled, and genuinely good people — and they were patient and generous about explaining everything, from the complex concepts to the decisions made long before I arrived. I took the dumb-question role gladly, and I learned an enormous amount — including how to learn, through them.

The approach

Advocating for the user

The approach was less a plan than a stance: advocate for the user, let go of what we thought we knew, and test it — learning along the way.

The product was an evolution. We couldn't have arrived at the end result from the beginning; it was never a case of “I thought about this, and this is the design.” It was continuous learning. What we did have was strong raw material — a great sound-sample library, solid engines, and genuinely smart people on the team. The missing piece was the user's perspective: how this actually had to be used by a beginner.

Early on we pictured a young audience — someone with no patience, born with computers, who takes everything for granted. That's not quite who we ended up serving, but designing for them forced the right questions. Because in the end it was less about features and more about understanding what makes a song a song: what someone needs to grasp to start building one, map it out, and let the technology help them — instead of making it for them.

Learning

“Looking good” with no depth wasn't helping

My biggest design enemy was a crisp 3D asset with no UX behind it. Stakeholders saw a cool 3D image, called it “cool,” and wanted it big on the screen — while the hierarchy that actually matters (typography, color, size) was off, and you couldn't tell what to do. Richer visuals are almost never the better solution; the details matter more than being obvious.
