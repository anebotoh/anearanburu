Rebuilding the creative core of Output Creator

A two-and-a-half-year redesign — rebuilt twice, 2,000+ screens — turning a tool built by music-production pros that no one could use into a play-first app for beginners. Led end to end as Lead Product Designer, through a Yamaha partnership and demos at NAMM and SXSW.

01 — The project

Redesigning a creative workflow

Output Creator is a desktop app that lets musicians create through sound exploration and play — record instruments or vocals, build original songs from a responsive, royalty-free sound palette, then share or export in a click.

I joined in January 2024. Over two and a half years I redesigned the app twice, drew more than 2,000 screens, tested countless prototypes — and became genuinely annoying to friends and family, running ideas past anyone who would listen.

The thinking underneath the 1.5 — the version that shipped — was solid. But somewhere along the way, competing opinions, designing by committee, and no user testing meant it didn't land with its audience. The company had spent more than a year building it. There was no product designer on the team, and it was built by pro music-production engineers for an audience of beginners.

“It doesn't matter if it looks cool if no one understands it.”

Me, to people who just hired me from a cool agency

02 — My role

Ignorance is bliss

I was hired partly because I didn't know music production. I'm a lifelong music junkie and studied classical music when I was younger, but I'd downloaded Ableton three or four times and given up every time. On this project, not knowing was one of my best assets — the people who built it knew it too well to see why no one else could use it.

As Lead Product Designer I owned the product design end to end — from workflow architecture and interaction design through the visual system and microinteractions. I set the design direction, ran critiques, and drove alignment across a cross-functional squad, reporting into Neil Halimen (VP Creative) and partnering closely with Daniel Lepik on 3D.

A year of decisions had accumulated with no product-design lens. So I spent the first month stripping them back and building a point of view — cataloguing the usability, visual-design, interaction, and information-architecture problems one by one. Then we got everyone in a room, in person, to agree on a direction before I designed a single new screen.

Being a beginner in music production while designing a tool for beginners meant I was basically my own experiment. I was learning as I designed — asking every dumb question you can think of.

And that's where teamwork mattered. Coming from the agency world, I learned at Output what collaboration actually means. I took the dumb-question role gladly, and I learned an enormous amount — including how to learn, through them.

03 — The approach

Advocating for the user

The approach was less a plan than a stance: advocate for the user, let go of what we thought we knew, and test it — learning along the way.

The product was an evolution. We couldn't have arrived at the end result from the beginning; it was never a case of “I thought about this, and this is the design.” It was continuous learning. What we did have was strong raw material — a great sound-sample library, solid engines, and genuinely smart people on the team. The missing piece was the user's perspective: how this actually had to be used by a beginner.

Early on we pictured a young audience — someone with no patience, born with computers, who takes everything for granted. That's not quite who we ended up serving, but designing for them forced the right questions. Because in the end it was less about features and more about understanding what makes a song a song: what someone needs to grasp to start building one, map it out, and let the technology help them — instead of making it for them.

04 — Key decisions

The calls that shaped the product

Decision 01

“Looking good” with no depth wasn't helping

My biggest design enemy was a crisp 3D asset with no UX behind it. Stakeholders saw a cool 3D image, called it “cool,” and wanted it big on the screen — while the hierarchy that actually matters (typography, color, size) was off, and you couldn't tell what to do. Richer visuals are almost never the better solution; the details matter more than being obvious.

Decision 02

A good onboarding won't fix a UX problem

When I joined, the team was “improving the onboarding” to fix retention — our metric was first-two-day retention. We tried prompting (“what do you want to create?”), sound “starters,” and idea starters. The learning: beginners don't know what to prompt. They'd type three words, usually lifted from the examples we showed. Asking a beginner “what do you want?” doesn't help — you have to give them a framework that's easy to understand and fun to use. And making an app “smart” is far harder than it sounds; our smart-composition experiments proved it.

Decision 03

Get to the magic moment as fast as possible

We realized the first magic moment was the instant you layered two sounds and a vibe appeared. The whole job was getting people there sooner. We built song ideas — a modal that mixed a few sounds into an idea you could drop onto the timeline. Gabriel, one of our back-end engineers, wrote an algorithm to match complementary sounds — and the trick was to make it imperfect. On a 1–100 scale, the sweet spot was 35. At 99% the song was basically made for you, and that was boring. Imperfection left room to play.

Why it workedThere's a difference between making something easy and making it feel like it's yours. The imperfection is what made it feel like yours.

Decision 04

From instrument categories to sound functions

One of the decisions I'm proudest of: we changed the IA from instrument categories to sound-function categories. Instrument categories quietly told a beginner they had to already know something — an instrument, a genre, a sound type — before they could start. Organizing by what a sound does let anyone begin, no vocabulary required.

Decision 05

No sound titles — the ear as a guide

We went back and forth on whether to show sample names at all. My instinct was clean, scannable lists with solid IA. The PM was certain we should lead with sound, not names, so people build by ear instead of reading. He was right — and I'll say so plainly: his call was better than mine. Leading with sound made the app more fun and easier for every audience.

The lessonLeading well includes knowing when someone else's call beats yours — and getting out of its way.

Decision 06

Starters became dots

Instead of loading long lists of sounds per collection, we grouped them by sound type and surfaced them as dots on the first screen — a way to start an idea or a section, not the entire workflow at once. Paired with the one rule that held everything together — color means sound — the first screen went from intimidating to inviting.

05 — The audience

One app, three musicians

Creator has always served three kinds of musician — but who we designed for first changed over the course of the project. The beginner needs it to be easy enough to build a song and gain confidence with the language of music. The instrumentalist wants to record in seconds and have the palette match their take. And the pro just needs to sketch an idea fast and export stems into their own DAW.

At the very beginning, we focused almost entirely on the beginner. About a year and a half in, a partnership with Yamaha shifted the emphasis — their audience is full of instrumentalists, so the target moved toward musicians who already play but are new to producing. That's what the latest version responds to.

None of that was a one-time decision — the audience kept moving with the business, and we kept testing it. We interviewed people constantly, checking three things at once: whether there was real product–market fit, whether the thing was actually usable, and whether what we were trying to communicate through the design was landing the way we intended.

Perfection made it boring.

07 — Leadership & collaboration

Reducing friction across the org

Design leadership here was as much about the organization as the screens. A lot of the job was undoing inherited decisions and working around a rigid back-end IA — early on the app had to follow the broken logic of the system it fed from, and I had to earn the room to break those rules and build on top of them — all while the brand was mid-rework and the product was in constant discovery.

I leaned on AI throughout: prototyping concepts and mash-ups, pressure-testing workflow decisions, and shipping front-end edits in Cursor. And I helped carry the work into the world — presenting and demoing at NAMM and SXSW with Yamaha, watching strangers use the app, have fun with it, and show us exactly where it broke.

Three unexpected ironies

  1. Irony 01

    Design vs. Design

    Just because something looks crisp doesn't mean it's understandable. The 1.0 leaned on crisp 3D assets that made the product look shiny and finished. But the moment you needed to actually understand it — how to navigate, how to link ideas — it fell apart, mostly because the visual hierarchy was working against the task. Those high-fidelity assets pulled focus from the core experience and made simply getting to the sounds harder. It looked resolved, and that was exactly the problem.

  2. Irony 02

    Perfect sounds made it boring.

    We tested a lot of variables and algorithms for matching sounds, and kept finding the same thing: the closer the match got to perfect, the more boring it felt — the song was basically made for you. We landed on a randomized approach we could tune, and dialed the parameters to a sweet spot where the results stayed surprising and the app became genuinely fun to use.

  3. Irony 03

    Lists help you navigate — but taking the typography away set it free.

    We started out wanting long lists of samples and categories. The big fight was about removing the titles from the sounds — so people wouldn't know what they were listening to. But that constraint became the whole concept of the app: instead of reading labels and leaning on categories, you use your ears and match sounds. Losing the words is what made it feel like music, not a database.

08 — What I learned

Designing for something invisible

Music is invisible. I was designing for abstract meaning — for the conversation between a piece of technology and a musician — constantly deciding how much the computer should do for you, and what creativity even means for a beginner. How do you encourage someone without doing it all for them? (At one point the honest question on the table was literally “how do you manage silence?” — we copy-pasted silence.)

A lot of the value was getting paid to ask the dumb questions with no shame: what does “intuitive” actually mean? what makes a design “smart”? Nothing was ever finished — it was continuous discovery. Set a vision, form a hypothesis, test it, fail, learn, repeat.

And I think the truest measure of Creator is what happens after it. It's a place to start, gain confidence, and graduate — to more advanced tools, to Output's other products, or, through Yamaha's program for instrumentalists, to a real path into producing your own music. It opens the door and points you somewhere good.

09 — Impact

What changed

Impact 01

Aligning builders and stakeholders behind a new audience

The company had built its tools for pros and now wanted to open a beginners' market — which raised a real fear about how that would land with existing customers. A lot of the work was getting everyone, builders and stakeholders alike, aligned and genuinely agreeing on what we were building. That alignment took enormous effort, and it's what let the product move forward.

Impact 02

A partnership that positioned the product

The work earned a partnership with Yamaha — putting Output and Creator in the company of a legendary, globally trusted brand. They invited us to present and demo Creator at NAMM and SXSW, inside their own offering: an endorsement that told the market Creator was to be taken seriously.

Impact 03

A tool that's easy, fun, and builds confidence

Above all, the outcome is a music-production tool that's genuinely easy, fun, and engaging — one that teaches you how a song is actually made. It builds confidence: the sense that you understand the parts worth paying attention to, that you can build and finish your first song, and have fun doing it.
